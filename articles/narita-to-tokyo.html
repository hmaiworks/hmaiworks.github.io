Narita to Tokyo: The 4 Ways Compared by Price, Time and Stress

Pocket Japan Guides · Airports · 8 min read · Facts checked against official operator information, November 2026

You have survived the flight, the immigration queue and the baggage carousel. Then the arrivals doors open at Narita Airport and you meet the wall of signs: Narita Express, Skyliner, Access Express, limousine bus, taxi. Every sign is confident. None of them knows where your hotel is.

That last sentence is the whole secret. Narita sits roughly 60 km east of central Tokyo, so the ride in takes an hour or more — and the "best" option is never a single answer. It depends on three things: which part of Tokyo your hotel is in, how much luggage you are dragging, and what time you land. This article compares the four ways in on price, time and stress, then gives you the shortcut that picks the right one in about ten seconds.

Way 1: The express trains — fast, but pick by neighbourhood

Narita has two express trains, and choosing between them is not about which is "better." It is about which side of Tokyo your hotel is on.

The Narita Express (N'EX), run by JR East, is the comfortable all-rounder. Every seat is reserved, there is dedicated luggage space, and it runs without a change to Tokyo Station in as little as about 53 minutes, then on to Shinagawa, Shibuya, Shinjuku and Yokohama. Expect around ¥3,140 to Tokyo Station and around ¥3,330 to Shinagawa, Shibuya or Shinjuku. If you hold a non-Japanese passport, the N'EX TOKYO Round Trip Ticket (around ¥5,200, valid 14 days) can make the return leg cheaper.

The Keisei Skyliner is the fastest train out of Narita: from about 36 minutes to Nippori and about 41 minutes to Keisei-Ueno, for around ¥2,580 — a little cheaper if you buy online in advance. All seats are reserved, trains run about every 20 minutes in the daytime, and Nippori is a simple change onto the JR Yamanote loop line. For hotels around Ueno, Nippori and Asakusa, and the north and east side of the loop, the Skyliner is usually the winner.

One warning that applies to both: an IC card alone is not enough for these trains. You also need the reserved-seat ticket, bought at the counter, a machine, or online. It takes two minutes — unless you try to do it as the train is leaving.

Way 2: Regular trains — half the price, a bit more work

The budget route is the ordinary commuter railway. The Keisei Access Express uses the same tracks as the Skyliner but stops more and has no reserved seats, for roughly ¥1,200–¥1,450. Many of its trains run straight through onto the subway Asakusa Line, reaching Asakusa or Nihombashi in roughly 55–75 minutes without a change — quietly one of the best-value rides in this article if your hotel is on that side of town. JR's regular rapid trains toward Tokyo Station cost around ¥1,400 and take roughly 75–90 minutes.

The trade-off is comfort, not safety or complexity: you keep your suitcase beside you in a normal carriage, and at busy times you may stand for part of the ride. For a solo traveller with one bag, that is a perfectly good deal. For a family arriving at rush hour with three suitcases, read on.

Way 3: The bus — the low-stress answer nobody expects

If "change trains with a suitcase" already tires you out, look at the buses. Staff load your bags into the hold, you sit down exactly once, and you get off at a major station — or, on many routes, right in front of a large hotel.

The Airport Limousine Bus connects Narita with major stations and hotels across Tokyo for roughly ¥3,100–¥3,600, taking about 75–120 minutes depending on traffic, and you can usually bring two checked bags per person. The low-cost TYO-NRT bus reaches Tokyo Station and Ginza for around ¥1,500 in roughly 65–80 minutes — express-train comfort trade-offs at a regular-train price. Buy your ticket at the bus counter in arrivals and let the staff point you to the right stop.

The honest caveat: roads have traffic and trains do not. In rush hour or bad weather, add time to every bus figure above. What the bus sells you is not speed; it is the absence of decisions between the arrivals hall and your hotel door.

Way 4: The taxi — simple, direct, and priced like it

A taxi is the simplest option of all: walk to the rank outside arrivals, show the driver your hotel's name and address (on your phone is fine), and ride to the door. Both of Tokyo's airports offer flat-rate taxis to central Tokyo, priced by area in advance — from Narita, expect roughly ¥23,500–¥38,500 per car, with expressway tolls on top and higher fares late at night (roughly 22:00–05:00). A taxi carries up to four passengers, though the boot fits only one or two large suitcases.

From Narita, treat the taxi as the late-night or group option: split between four travellers, the per-person cost lands near the express trains, with none of the walking. Solo, after a long flight, it is a luxury you should choose with your eyes open — always ask for the flat rate at the rank before you ride.

The trap: last trains leave earlier than you think

This is the part brochures skip, and the part that strands first-time visitors. Tokyo's trains stop for the night, and Narita's stop early. The last N'EX leaves Narita at around 21:44. The last Skyliner leaves at around 23:00, and late TYO-NRT buses to Tokyo Station run until around 23:30 (at a higher fare of around ¥3,000). After that, it is a taxi or a night near the airport.

And landing time is not leaving time: immigration and baggage can take 30–60 minutes. If your flight is scheduled to land within about 90 minutes of your last departure, decide your backup plan before you fly — a delayed flight plus a slow queue is exactly how people end up paying for the ¥30,000 taxi at midnight.

The luggage trick: you do not have to carry your suitcase at all. Both of Tokyo's airports have luggage delivery counters in arrivals (look for TA-Q-BIN), where a large suitcase can be sent to your Tokyo hotel for roughly ¥2,400–¥3,600 on standard, usually next-day, delivery. Send the big bag ahead, ride whichever train suits your hotel, and travel with a day bag. Your hotel must be able to receive it — tell them it is coming, and put your reservation name on the form.

The 10-second shortcut: choose by hotel area

  • Tokyo Station, Ginza, Nihombashi: N'EX, or the TYO-NRT bus on a budget.
  • Shinjuku, Shibuya, Ikebukuro: N'EX direct — no change, no thinking.
  • Ueno, Nippori, Asakusa: Skyliner for speed, Access Express for value.
  • Shinagawa: N'EX direct.
  • Yokohama: N'EX direct.
  • Disney Resort area or Odaiba: a direct airport bus saves awkward changes.
  • Landing late, or travelling as a group with heavy bags: bus while they run — then the flat-rate taxi, split.

Notice what the list never says: "take the famous train." The fastest train to the wrong side of Tokyo still leaves you a long extra ride at the far end. Start from your hotel's nearest station and work backwards — that one habit is worth more than any single fare saving in this article.

The bottom line

For most first-time visitors, the answer is the express train that serves your hotel's neighbourhood — Skyliner for the east and north, N'EX for the centre, west and south. The bus wins when luggage or children make changes expensive in energy. Regular trains win on price when you pack light. And the taxi wins at midnight, shared. Pick by hotel, luggage and landing time, and the ride into Tokyo becomes what it should be: the first page of the trip, not an exam at the end of a long flight.
