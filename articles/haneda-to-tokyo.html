Haneda to Tokyo: Keikyu vs Monorail — Which One Actually Fits Your Hotel?

Pocket Japan Guides · Airports · 7 min read · Facts checked against official operator information, November 2026

Haneda is the kind airport. Unlike Narita, which sits roughly 60 km from central Tokyo, Haneda is inside the city itself — most central areas are 30 to 50 minutes away, and the nearest big hub is barely ten minutes by train. So the arrivals hall offers you a gentle problem instead of a hard one: two railways, the Keikyu Line and the Tokyo Monorail, both quick, both cheap, both leaving every few minutes.

Because both are good, first-time visitors tend to pick by whichever sign they see first. That works — until it adds an awkward extra change at the far end. The two lines go to different gateways into Tokyo, so the right choice is decided by one thing: where your hotel is. Here is the comparison, the shortcut, and the options for when trains are not the answer.

The short answer

Take the Keikyu Line if your hotel is near Shinagawa, on the Asakusa side of town, or in Yokohama — it is the cheaper ride (around ¥330 to Shinagawa) and many of its trains run straight through to Asakusa with no change. Take the Tokyo Monorail if you are heading for Hamamatsucho, Tokyo Station or the east side of the Yamanote loop — it ends at one simple JR terminus, which makes the onward connection almost impossible to get wrong. Neither needs a reservation: an IC card is all you need for both.

Keikyu: the cheaper line with the longer reach

The Keikyu Line (the red trains) is the value pick and, for many hotels, the direct pick. From Terminal 3 it reaches Shinagawa in about 11 minutes for around ¥330. Shinagawa is a major JR hub: change there for the Yamanote loop to Shibuya, Shinjuku and Tokyo Station, or for the Shinkansen if you are leaving Tokyo the same day.

The part most visitors miss: many Keikyu trains do not stop being useful at Shinagawa. They continue onto the subway Asakusa Line, so you can ride directly to Asakusa in roughly 35 minutes for around ¥610 — no change, no second fare calculation, your IC card handles it. If your hotel is in Asakusa, Nihombashi or anywhere along that diagonal, this through train is quietly the easiest ride from Haneda. Keikyu is also the line for Yokohama, about 25 minutes away, with no change at all.

One small trap: at the airport station, Keikyu runs trains in two directions — toward Shinagawa and Tokyo, and toward Yokohama. Check the destination on the board before you board. A train to Yokohama is a lovely ride, but only if Yokohama is where you sleep.

The Monorail: the simplest line with the friendliest terminus

The Tokyo Monorail glides above the bay to Hamamatsucho in about 13 minutes on the fastest trains, for around ¥520, with trains every few minutes. The ride itself is part of the pleasure — water, runways and the city sliding closer — but the real advantage is at the far end.

Hamamatsucho is a single, manageable JR station on the Yamanote loop. There is one line to find, in two directions, and from it Tokyo Station and Ueno are a few stops one way, Shinagawa a few stops the other. For a tired first-timer, "one terminus, one loop line" is a genuinely simpler mental model than a through-running subway. The Monorail also suits hotels around Hamamatsucho, Daimon and the Tokyo Tower side of town, which are walkable or one short stop away.

What the Monorail does not do: go anywhere else. Every journey continues on JR. If that onward change sounds like friction, compare honestly with Keikyu's through trains before you decide by the view.

When the answer is neither: bus and taxi

Trains win on price and predictability, but they still ask you to navigate a station with your bags. Two alternatives earn their place:

The Airport Limousine Bus connects Haneda with major stations and hotels for roughly ¥1,200–¥1,400, taking about 25–60 minutes depending on traffic. Staff load your luggage (usually up to two checked bags per person), you sit down once, and many routes stop at or near large hotels. With children, big suitcases or heavy jet lag, "no changes" is worth more than the fare difference.

A taxi from Haneda is the one airport taxi ride in Tokyo that can be a reasonable treat rather than a budget event. Flat-rate taxis to central Tokyo are priced by area in advance — expect roughly ¥7,600–¥9,100 to central wards in the daytime (the full table runs to about ¥14,000 for the furthest areas), with expressway tolls on top and higher fares late at night (roughly 22:00–05:00). A taxi carries up to four passengers, though the boot fits only one or two large suitcases. Split between three or four travellers after a long flight, the per-person cost approaches a modest premium over train-plus-changes — with a door-to-door ride at the end of it.

The luggage trick: you do not have to carry your suitcase at all. Haneda's arrivals halls have luggage delivery counters (look for TA-Q-BIN), where a large suitcase can be sent to your Tokyo hotel for roughly ¥2,400–¥3,600 on standard, usually next-day, delivery. Same-day delivery exists from Haneda but has a late-morning cut-off (around 11:00) and costs more — ¥5,170 for a large suitcase to central Tokyo. Your hotel must be able to receive the bag, so tell them it is coming and put your reservation name on the form. Send the big bag ahead, and either train becomes a light, easy ride.

Landing late? Haneda forgives — up to a point

Haneda's late-night picture is kinder than Narita's, but it still ends. Keikyu trains run until just after midnight (last train to Shinagawa at around 00:08), and the Monorail until around midnight. After the trains, late-night buses to the Shinjuku and Ikebukuro areas leave Terminal 3 until around 02:20, for around ¥2,800. Beyond that, it is a taxi.

And remember: landing time is not leaving time. Immigration and baggage can take 30–60 minutes, so a 23:30 landing is really a 00:15 arrival in the city-access sense. If your flight lands close to midnight, decide on the bus or taxi before you fly, not at the gate with a dying phone.

The 10-second shortcut: choose by hotel area

  • Shinagawa: Keikyu direct — the easiest airport ride in Tokyo.
  • Asakusa, Nihombashi: a Keikyu through train, no change.
  • Yokohama: Keikyu direct.
  • Tokyo Station, Ueno, the east side of the loop: Monorail to Hamamatsucho, then JR.
  • Shinjuku, Shibuya, Ikebukuro: either line plus one JR change — Keikyu via Shinagawa is usually the smoother path; a direct bus removes the change entirely.
  • Big luggage, small children, deep jet lag: the Limousine Bus, or a flat-rate taxi split between you.
  • Landing near midnight: whichever train is still running — then the late-night bus, then a taxi.

The bottom line

Keikyu or Monorail is not a contest with a winner; it is a matching exercise. Keikyu reaches further and costs less, and its through trains to Asakusa are the best-kept secret of Haneda arrivals. The Monorail trades reach for simplicity, ending at one friendly JR terminus on the loop. Start from your hotel's nearest station, work backwards, and Haneda's famous kindness extends all the way to your check-in desk.
