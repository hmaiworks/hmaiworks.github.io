Welcome Suica vs Suica Mobile: Which IC Card Should a First-Time Visitor Actually Get?

Pocket Japan Guides · Transport · 7 min read · Facts checked against JR East, November 2026

Almost every first trip to Japan starts with the same small panic. You have landed, you are tired, and you are standing in front of a ticket machine with a line forming behind you. Somewhere in the noise you have heard the word "Suica" — and now there seem to be three different versions of it, all asking for your money.

Here is the good news: the choice is simpler than it looks. There are exactly three realistic ways for a visitor to get a Suica, and one quick test tells you which one is yours. This article gives you that test, the real differences behind it, and the traps that catch first-time visitors most often.

The 30-second answer

Have an iPhone and a short trip? Set up Welcome Suica Mobile before you fly — no queue, no deposit, valid 180 days.

Have an Android phone bought outside Japan? Skip the apps entirely. Buy a physical Welcome Suica when you land — it works regardless of your phone.

Staying longer than 28 days, or likely to come back to Japan? Get a regular physical Suica instead. It has no expiry clock ticking against your trip, and its balance can be refunded.

Everything below is the detail behind those three lines — worth reading once, because two of the three options have a "money you cannot get back" clause hiding inside them.

All three work the same way once you have them: charge money on, tap the reader when you enter the ticket gate, tap again when you leave, and the fare is calculated for you. All three also work beyond trains — on buses, and in convenience stores, vending machines, coin lockers and restaurants that display the nationwide IC card compatibility mark. The maximum balance any of them can hold is ¥20,000.

The detail that matters most: the 28-day clock starts at the till

The single most misunderstood fact about the physical Welcome Suica is when its clock starts. The card is valid for 28 days including the date of purchase — not 28 days from your first ride. Buy it on a Monday for a trip that ends 30 days later, and the card dies before you do. On the 29th day it simply becomes invalid, and whatever balance remains on it is gone, because Welcome Suica balances cannot be refunded, whatever is left and however long is left on the clock.

This is not a reason to avoid the card — for a typical one- or two-week trip it is the easiest option there is, and there is no deposit to fuss over. It is a reason to do two things: buy it when you arrive rather than stockpiling it early in a long trip, and top up in small amounts as you go. JR East sells the card in set amounts from ¥1,000 to ¥10,000, and station machines top up in set amounts too (¥500, ¥1,000, ¥2,000, ¥3,000, ¥5,000 or ¥10,000). Small and often beats one big charge you cannot recover.

Welcome Suica Mobile: the best option, if your phone qualifies

Welcome Suica Mobile is JR East's app for iOS. Instead of queueing at the airport, you download the app, agree to the terms, set a secret keyword and turn on location services, and a Suica is issued inside the app. It is valid for 180 days from the date of issue — far more forgiving than the physical card's 28 — and you top it up with a card registered in Apple Pay. There is no deposit. You must be 13 or over, and your device must support Apple Pay.

Two catches, both worth knowing before you rely on it. First, the leftover balance is not refunded — when the 180 days end, unspent money expires, so the small-top-ups habit applies here too. Second, app download and top-up are restricted by law in some countries, so depending on where you live you may only be able to finish the setup after you arrive in Japan. Do the download at home if you can, but do not panic at the airport if the last step waits for Japanese soil.

The Android answer is short: buy the plastic card

If your phone is an Android bought outside Japan, this section saves you an hour of fighting with settings. There is no Android version of Welcome Suica Mobile. And Mobile Suica, JR East's Android service, requires an Osaifu-Keitai (FeliCa) device — a hardware feature most phones sold outside Japan do not have.

So for overseas Android users the answer is not a workaround; it is the physical card. That is genuinely fine. A physical Welcome Suica does everything the app does at the gate, needs no battery, and cannot be locked behind a dead phone at the far end of a long day. Plenty of iPhone users choose it for exactly those reasons.

When the regular Suica beats both visitor cards

The regular Suica is the unglamorous option, and for some trips it is the right one. It asks for a ¥500 deposit when you buy it — but that deposit comes back when you return the card, and so does your remaining balance, minus a ¥220 handling fee. A card returned with ¥1,000 left on it refunds ¥1,280: the ¥1,000, minus ¥220, plus the ¥500 deposit.

Choose the regular Suica if your trip runs longer than 28 days, if you visit Japan regularly and want a card that simply keeps working, or if the idea of an un-refundable balance bothers you more than a small deposit does. One caution from the official rules: a lost card cannot be reissued, so treat it like the cash it effectively is.

Where to buy the physical cards

Physical Welcome Suica cards are sold at officially designated locations, including JR EAST Travel Service Centers at Narita Airport (Terminals 1 and 2·3), Haneda Airport Terminal 3, and major stations such as Tokyo, Shinjuku, Shibuya, Ikebukuro, Ueno and Yokohama, with Welcome Suica vending machines at the airport stations. Sales locations and the amounts on sale can change, so check the official JR East Welcome Suica page shortly before your trip rather than trusting any article — including this one — for the final list.

Four mistakes first-time visitors make with IC cards

  1. Loading a big balance onto a non-refundable card. On a Welcome Suica or Welcome Suica Mobile, unspent money is unrecoverable. Top up a day or two of travel at a time.
  2. Forgetting to tap out. The fare is calculated from both taps. Miss the exit tap and the trip never closes properly — staff can fix it, but it costs you time at the gate.
  3. Assuming one card can be shared. Each person needs their own card or phone. The tap-in/tap-out record is per person; sharing breaks it and can leave one of you stuck inside the gates.
  4. Assuming the card covers every train. IC cards work within one IC service area, and limited express trains and Green Cars need their own separate tickets on top of the IC fare. A cross-country shinkansen trip is a ticket purchase, not a tap.

So, which one should you get?

Run the test one more time. iPhone, short trip, happy to set up an app: Welcome Suica Mobile. Android from overseas, or you simply want zero setup: physical Welcome Suica at the airport. Long trip, repeat visits, or refund anxiety: regular Suica. Whichever you choose, the pattern from your first ride is identical — charge a little, tap in, tap out — and the ticket-machine panic from the start of this article never happens again.
